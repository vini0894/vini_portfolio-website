Case 05 · Experimentation

Paying only for sessions that actually happen.

Providers were paid for recurring sessions even when the user repeatedly didn't show. I ran a controlled experiment to stop the leak without punishing genuine re-engagement.

6.9→2.5% no-show rate, with paid no-shows in the test cohort dropping from 41% to 10%.

01 · Problem

One missed session quietly predicted the next.

In recurring bookings, a single no-show was a strong signal of more to come, yet the platform kept paying providers for sessions the user never attended. The cost compounded silently across every recurring plan.

75%miss rate on the next session after one no-show
41%of recurring sessions were paid no-shows at baseline
5moof data behind the analysis

02 · Method

Add one confirmation step, behind a controlled rollout.

After a no-show, the user's upcoming recurring sessions move to pending and require an explicit confirmation; unconfirmed sessions auto-cancel 24 hours ahead, so providers aren't booked for a ghost. Users get push and email nudges with one-tap confirmation.

Run as a backend, user-level experiment against a matched control, with guardrails on completion and re-engagement so the cost win couldn't quietly wreck the experience.

Trigger · after a no-show → Pending · confirm to keep → Auto-cancel · 24h prior

03 · Result

Far fewer paid no-shows, with the tradeoff measured.

No-show rate6.9 → 2.5%
Paid no-shows41 → 10%
Completion (tradeoff)37 → 21%

Requiring confirmation recovered meaningful monthly cost. It also lowered completion, because some users re-engage less after a miss, a tradeoff I measured rather than hid.

04 · Decision

Hand leadership a dial, not a verdict.

I framed it as a choice with the numbers attached: full rollout for maximum savings, or a selective rollout that protects booking volume. The job wasn't to pick for them, it was to make the tradeoff impossible to misread.

Every fix has a tradeoff. Name it before you ship.
